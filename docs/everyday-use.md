# Everyday use: getting value out of mycelium, from personal to advanced

Most of these docs assume you're a developer wiring memory into a coding agent.
You don't have to be. Mycelium is just as useful for the ordinary stuff
(finances, scheduling, research, studying, a home project, health admin,
writing), and it scales all the way up from there.

This page is the plain-language guide to *using* it. It starts with zero technical
knowledge required, then climbs through developer and advanced/power use so you
can see the whole range in one place. Read the top if you're new; keep going as
your needs grow.

---

## The one idea that changes everything

Without memory, every conversation starts from zero. You re-explain your
situation, your preferences, the people involved, what you decided last week.
It's like a brilliant assistant with no long-term memory who you re-onboard every
morning.

**With memory, Claude stops forgetting.** Tell it something once and it's there
next week, next month. Over time it stops feeling like a chatbot and starts
feeling like someone who knows your world. The value isn't any single answer;
it's that context *compounds*.

That shifts your job slightly. As well as asking questions, you also **tell it
things worth keeping**, and occasionally say *"remember that."* Small habit, big
payoff.

---

## Keep your worlds separate with "projects"

Different areas of your life shouldn't bleed together. Mycelium files memories
under a **project**. Just name the world you're in at the start of a chat:

> "This is for the **house move** project."
> "Working on **budgeting** today."
> "This is my **thesis** research."

Then what it remembers is scoped to that world, and when you return, it surfaces
*that* context and not the rest. You don't manage folders; you name the world.

Use a stable name per world (`"budgeting"`, not "the money thing") so it can find
the right context every time.

---

## What's worth telling it to remember

The rule: **if you'd be annoyed to re-explain it, save it.** Say *"remember
this:"* then the fact. A few flavors:

**Facts about your situation**
- "Remember: my fiscal year ends June 30; I file quarterly."
- "Remember: our landlord is Priya; repairs go through the building manager, not her."
- "Remember: the recurring deadlines: insurance renews in September, car MOT in March."

**People and their quirks**
- "Remember: my accountant wants a spreadsheet, not a PDF, and mileage logged separately."
- "Remember the contractors: framing crew is great but slow; the tiler flaked twice."

**Decisions and rationale**
- "Remember: we chose the fixed-rate plan because we wanted predictable payments."
- "Remember: decided NOT to expand the deck this year; over budget."

**Your preferences (so answers fit you)**
- "Remember: short bullet answers, not essays."
- "Give me the number first, then the explanation."
- "Draft emails in a warm but brief tone."

You don't do this all at once; just *"remember that"* whenever something surfaces
that'll matter again.

---

## What NOT to save

- **Secrets:** passwords, full card numbers, government IDs, bank logins. Never.
  (Names and categories are fine; the actual credentials are not.)
- **One-off trivia** that won't come up again.
- **Things that change hourly,** like "the invoice is on my desk right now."
  That's today, not memory.

If unsure, lean toward saving; mycelium detects near-duplicates on save and asks
you to update instead of piling up noise.

## What a useful session actually looks like

**A recurring task (e.g. monthly budgeting)**
> "Budgeting project. Here are this month's transactions. Categorize them using
> the rules you remember and flag anything odd." → because it remembers your
> categories, you get consistent output every month instead of re-teaching it.

**Prep that spans time (e.g. tax or application prep)**
> "What did we agree to track this year, and what's still missing before I submit?"
> → it pulls up decisions you saved months ago so nothing slips.

**Planning against your own rules (e.g. a week)**
> "Plan my week respecting my constraints (no calls before 10, Fridays clear,
> pickup at 3:15)." → it already knows the constraints; you don't re-list them.

**A long project check-in (e.g. a move, a build, a course)**
> "Where are we, and what's the next thing blocking progress?" → it recalls the
> budget, the gating step, the open problems, and tells you what's next.

**Recurring life-admin**
> "What renewals or deadlines are coming up in the next 60 days?" → it surfaces
> whatever you told it once.

## A gallery: all kinds of people, all kinds of uses

Memory helps anywhere context piles up over time. A spread of real shapes:

**Freelancer / consultant**
Remember each client's rate, invoicing schedule, brand voice, and pet peeves.
→ "Draft the monthly update for Acme in their voice" just works, per client.

**Student**
Remember your course load, due dates, citation style, and your professor's
rubric. → "What's due this week and what's the next thing I should start?"

**Researcher / writer**
Remember your thesis, the sources you've read, and the argument you're building.
→ "Given what we've established, what's the weakest part of my argument?"

**Job hunt**
Remember the roles you've applied to, who you talked to, and your salary floor.
→ "Prep me for the Clearform interview; remind me where that one stalled."

**Parent / household manager**
Remember pickup times, allergies, the pediatrician's advice, the sitter's number,
who's coming to the birthday party. → "Plan Saturday around the kids' schedule."

**Personal finance / budgeting**
Remember your categories, recurring bills, savings goal, and which card is which.
→ "Categorize this month's spending and tell me if I'm on track for the goal."

**Small-business owner / bookkeeping**
Remember vendor-to-category rules, your fiscal year, and how your accountant wants
things. → monthly books close the same clean way every time.

**Home renovation / DIY**
Remember the trades, the budget line, permit numbers, and the inspection gates.
→ "Where are we on budget and what's blocking the next step?"

**Health & medical admin**
Remember your medications, doses, appointment history, and questions for the next
visit. → "Summarize my history for the new specialist and list what to ask."
(Save the *facts*, never portal passwords.)

**Caregiver**
Remember a parent's meds, providers, insurance details, and the running to-do.
→ "What's outstanding for Mom's care this week?"

**Travel planning**
Remember passport expiries, loyalty numbers, dietary needs, and who's coming.
→ "Plan the Portugal trip around everyone's constraints."

**Wedding / event planning**
Remember the guest list, vendors booked, budget, and open decisions.
→ "What's still undecided six weeks out?"

**Fitness / training**
Remember your program, injuries to work around, and your progress.
→ "Adjust this week's plan around my tweaked shoulder."

**Language / skill learning**
Remember your level, the words you keep missing, and your practice goal.
→ "Quiz me on the verbs I got wrong last time."

**Immigration / legal paperwork**
Remember which forms are filed, deadlines, and case/reference numbers.
→ "What's the next filing and by when?" (Reference numbers yes; ID scans no.)

**Landlord / property**
Remember each unit, tenant, lease dates, and maintenance history.
→ "Which leases renew this quarter and what needs fixing at Unit 3?"

**Hobbyist (garden, homebrew, collecting, music)**
Remember what you planted and when, recipes and results, your collection, songs
learned. → "What should go in the beds next month given what I planted in spring?"

**Volunteer / community organizer**
Remember roles, contacts, recurring events, and what worked last time.
→ "Plan the fall fundraiser using what we learned from the spring one."

**Anyone who writes a lot of email**
Remember your tone and standard positions. → every draft sounds like you, not
like a robot, without you re-specifying.

The pattern is always the same: **tell it the durable facts once, and every future
request in that world starts warm instead of cold.**

## Climbing the ladder: technical and advanced use

The same tool scales all the way up. The everyday examples above are the ground
floor; here's the rest of the building.

### Technical / developer use

If you use Claude (or any MCP client) to write code, memory becomes your agent's
long-term knowledge of the codebase:

- **Project facts:** "the API lives in `/server`, the port is 8080, auth is
  handled in `middleware/auth.ts`." Stop re-explaining the layout every session.
- **Bugs and their fixes:** "the flaky test was a timezone bug; fixed by pinning
  UTC in the fixture." Next time it recurs, the fix is one recall away.
- **Conventions and decisions:** "we use pnpm not npm; we chose Postgres over
  Mongo because of X." The rationale survives past the person who made it.
- **Cross-tool continuity:** a lesson saved in your Claude session is visible to
  your Codex session. The memory is the agent's, not any one CLI's.
- **The habit, automated:** paste [claude-md-primer.md](claude-md-primer.md)
  into your `CLAUDE.md` so the agent recalls before asking and saves what's worth
  keeping, every session, without being told.

→ Bootstrap an existing codebase into memory with the recipes in
[seeding.md](seeding.md) (scan a project tree, import your `CLAUDE.md`/`AGENTS.md`,
distill shell history).

### Advanced / power use

Once you're relying on it, these are the levers:

- **One shared brain across many clients:** point Claude Code, Claude Desktop,
  and Codex at the same store so they all draw on (and add to) one memory. See
  [Multi-client mechanics](../README.md#multi-client-mechanics).
- **Turn on semantic recall:** match by *meaning*, not just keywords, via any
  OpenAI-compatible embeddings endpoint (local Ollama/LM Studio or cloud). See
  [Optional: semantic recall](../README.md#optional-semantic-recall) and prove the
  lift on your own data with [benchmarks.md](benchmarks.md).
- **Consolidation:** periodically merge a cluster of hot, related memories into
  one dense "cold" summary, then forget the originals. Keeps the network sharp
  instead of bloated. See [concepts.md](concepts.md#lifecycle--what-happens-to-a-memory-over-time).
- **Pinning & resolvers:** `pin` facts you've confirmed so their connections
  never decay; seed a `resolver` for an ambiguous term so future recalls
  disambiguate automatically. See [concepts.md](concepts.md).
- **Recency & prior-override:** surface what just happened (episodic `recent()` /
  `/wake`) and let a stored fact outrank the model's training assumptions with a
  salience flag. See [recency-precision.md](recency-precision.md).
- **Behavioral memory (foundry):** an append-only log of decisions
  ("picked the cheaper model tier for this prompt size"), queryable later for
  pattern analysis or training-data extraction. See
  [concepts.md](concepts.md#foundry--behavioral-memory).
- **Offload upkeep to a local LLM:** have a small local model draft consolidation
  summaries and an archive/keep proposal for you to approve, so maintenance
  doesn't cost tokens or attention. See
  [local-llm-maintenance.md](local-llm-maintenance.md).
- **It's just files:** one SQLite DB for memories, one for decisions, one TOML
  config. Back it up, version it, sync it, or run it on a private network you
  control. See [configuration.md](configuration.md) and
  [Backups](../README.md#backups).

Same tool, same store; you just reach for more of it as your needs grow.

## The two habits that make it pay off

**1. Seed it early.** Spend 20 minutes up front telling it the durable facts:
the people, deadlines, decisions, your preferences. End with *"remember all of
this."* That one session is what makes every later one feel like it already knows
you. (For power users, [seeding.md](seeding.md) has structured recipes.)

**2. Checkpoint the long stuff.** For anything spanning days, when you stop, save
a bookmark of where you are:

> `/checkpoint`, or just say *"save a checkpoint of where we are before I go."*

Next time, Claude reads it first and picks up mid-stride instead of asking "what
were we doing?" See [concepts.md](concepts.md#session-checkpoints).

---

## A gentle weekly rhythm (optional)

- **Start of week:** "What's due or scheduled this week?" Let it front-load things.
- **As you go:** whenever a durable fact surfaces, *"remember that."*
- **End of week:** "Anything I said I'd follow up on that's still open?" plus
  a checkpoint on anything unfinished.

Ten seconds here and there, and within a month Claude knows your world well enough
to take work off your plate instead of just answering trivia.

---

## If it ever feels like it forgot something

- Nudge it: "check your memory, I told you my categories."
- If it's matching only exact words and missing your meaning, the optional
  semantic-recall model may be off; that's a graceful fallback, and nothing is
  lost. See [Troubleshooting](../README.md#troubleshooting) and
  [Optional: semantic recall](../README.md#optional-semantic-recall).

Everything you save lives on **your** machine, in a file. It's yours, it doesn't
go anywhere, and it's still there tomorrow.
